Physical properties

What makes a metal a metal

Metals are elements that lose electrons easily and form positive ions. That one fact explains almost every property below.

Lustre

They shine

A fresh metal surface reflects light, which is why gold, silver and copper became ornament metals.

Malleability

They beat into sheets

Aluminium foil and corrugated roofing sheets are made this way.

Ductility

They draw into wires

Copper and silver are the most ductile metals we use.

Conductivity

They carry heat and current

Free electrons drift through the metal — hence copper wires and aluminium cooking pots.

Sonority

They ring when struck

School bells and temple bells are cast from metal alloys.

Hardness

Mostly hard, dense, high melting

Sodium and potassium are the soft exceptions; mercury is liquid at room temperature.

Chemical properties

How metals behave in reactions

Metals give electrons away, so they act as reducing agents and their oxides are basic.

Three reactions to remember

With oxygen — forms a basic oxide4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O
With dilute acid — releases hydrogen gasZn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
Displacement — a reactive metal pushes out a weaker oneFe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu

Step 1

Mining

Mining is the process of taking ores containing metals out of the Earth's crust. Which method is used depends on one thing above all: how deep the ore lies.

Surface or open-cast mining

Used when the ore lies at or near the surface. The soil and waste rock above it — the overburden — is stripped off, and the ore body is cut away in wide steps called benches. It is cheaper, faster and safer than digging shafts, but it scars a large area of land.

BauxiteIron oreLimestone

Underground mining

Used when the ore lies deep below the surface. Vertical shafts and horizontal tunnels are driven down to the ore body, and the ore is blasted loose and hauled up. It is costly and risky, and needs constant ventilation and support, but it reaches ore no pit could.

GoldCopperCoal
  • Prospecting first — geologists survey the rock and test samples before any digging starts, to check the deposit is rich enough to pay for itself
  • Placer mining — a third, simpler method in which heavy metal grains such as gold are washed out of river sand and gravel
  • Ore comes out impure — it always arrives mixed with gangue, the worthless sand, clay and rock around it

Mining leaves real damage behind: pits and waste heaps, dust, noise, and acid water draining from exposed sulphide rock. Modern mines are expected to refill pits and replant the land once the ore runs out.

Step 2

Grinding, or crushing and pulverisation

Ore leaves the mine as boulders. Nothing can be separated from a boulder, so it is broken down in stages until it is a powder finer than sand.

Stage 1

Crushing

Jaw and cone crushers squeeze the large lumps between hard steel plates and break them into pieces the size of gravel.

Stage 2

Pulverising

Ball mills and stamp mills tumble or pound those pieces until the ore becomes a fine, free-flowing powder.

Stage 3

Sieving

Screens let powder of the right size through and send anything still too coarse back to the mill.

Why the ore must be ground

  • More surface area — small particles expose far more surface for air, water and chemicals to act on, so roasting and leaching go faster
  • Frees the mineral — grinding breaks the bond between the valuable mineral and the gangue locked around it, so the two can then be separated
  • Makes concentration possible — froth flotation and magnetic separation only work on loose, fine particles
  • Easier handling — powder can be poured, pumped as a slurry and fed evenly into a furnace

The equipment

Jaw crusherBreaks boulders into small lumps

Grinding costs a great deal of electricity, so mills grind only as fine as the next step truly needs — never finer.

Step 4

Roasting and calcination

Both turn the concentrated ore into an oxide. The difference is simply how much air is allowed in.

Roasting — in excess air

Sulphide ores are heated strongly below their melting point. Sulphides become oxides, and volatile impurities such as sulphur, arsenic and antimony escape.

Zinc blende2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂
Lead sulphide2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂

Calcination — in little or no air

Carbonate and hydrated ores are heated below melting point. Water and carbon dioxide are driven off, leaving the ore porous and easy to reduce.

Limestone → quicklimeCaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
Calamine → zinc oxideZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂
Bauxite → aluminaAl₂O₃·2H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 2H₂O

Steps 5 and 6

Smelting and electrorefining

First the metal is pulled out of its oxide, then it is purified.

Smelting

The concentrated ore is heated with a reducing agent — carbon, carbon monoxide or another metal — in a blast or reverberatory furnace.

Iron in a blast furnaceFe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
Tin extractionSnO₂ + C → Sn + CO₂

Electrorefining

Electrolysis purifies the metal. The impure metal is the anode, pure metal the cathode, and a salt of the metal such as CuSO₄ or AgNO₃ the electrolyte.

At the anode — impure copper dissolvesCu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻
At the cathode — pure copper is depositedCu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
